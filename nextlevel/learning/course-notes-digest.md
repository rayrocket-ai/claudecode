Source: Ray's own document "Automation Incubator Course Notes", dated 2026-11-14, read in full on 2026-11-15 (one heading, an opening paragraph and seven sections, about 2,400 words). This digest is written in our own words. It does not copy the course's text, scripts or templates. The course belongs to its author. Next Level is not connected to the course or its author.

How to read the "confidence" notes: high means the notes are specific and consistent; medium means the notes give the idea and leave out detail; low means the notes are thin, unverified or point to paid material Ray has not seen.

## The whole model in 10 lines

1. Sell ready-made automations to local businesses as a setup fee plus a monthly retainer.
2. Run everything on one platform (HighLevel): CRM, forms, calendars, messaging, payments, AI agents, workflows.
3. An automation is a trigger, then actions, with logic and waits in between.
4. Follow six stages: get the platform, set up the brand, learn to build, sell, onboard and collect, fulfil and keep the retainer.
5. The first contact has one goal: book a call. The selling happens on a discovery call that finds the pain, presents the fix and tries to close.
6. The menu is seven automations: speed to lead, voice AI caller, website chat, appointment reminders, review requests, social DM replies, lead nurture.
7. Start with people you already know, add one outbound channel that fits the niche, then build inbound content and partnerships once there are results to show.
8. Stop selling custom builds. Standardize one automation, make onboarding self-serve, price it monthly, and grow one product before starting a second.
9. Keep three sub-accounts: your own business, a demo, and the first client. Give clients limited access.
10. The free lessons teach finding and closing clients far more than building. The builds, and much of the detail, sit in paid material the notes do not cover.

## Section 2. Foundations

**The idea.** An automation is a trigger followed by actions, with logic in between. Any local business with customers and appointments can use one.

**The worked example (structure only).** A booking triggers a confirmation by email and text, the business record updates, the workflow waits a day, a reminder goes out, and the customer either confirms or goes into a reschedule sequence.

**Who it suits, per the notes.** Barbers, gyms, clinics, home services, and brokers, with real estate and mortgage named.

**Quick-win automations listed.** Booking confirmations and reminders, review requests, lead capture with nurture and appointment setting, social auto-replies and DM bots, invoicing, AI chat widgets and voice receptionists, social post scheduling.

**The tool, two routes.**
- White-label HighLevel yourself: quoted at US$300 or $500 a month, your own brand, DNS work, 1 to 2 weeks to go live.
- License the author's white-labelled copy: quoted at about $97 a month, faster, with pre-built automations, a client onboarding course, a set of custom AI assistants and three sub-accounts to start. A snapshot lets you move out later.
- The notes add Ray's own observation: he already runs HighLevel, so the licence would add templates and assistants, not a new capability.

**The workflow builder, the parts that matter.**
- Triggers: contact created or changed, tag added, form submitted, appointment booked, customer replied, call status, pipeline stage changed, invoice or payment event. One workflow can have several.
- Actions: tags, field updates, notes, email, text, voicemail or call, internal notifications that use custom values such as first name.
- Logic: if/else, wait (time, event or reply), go-to, drip, math, remove from workflow.
- Settings: allow re-entry, stop on response, sending time windows.
- Testing: save, publish, test with a sample contact. A manual test skips the trigger conditions. Enrollment history and execution logs show where a contact stalled.

**Second worked example (structure only).** Review request: job completed, a text asks for a rating from 1 to 5, low ratings go privately to management, high ratings receive the public review link, with follow-ups.

**Platform advice.** Ignore memberships, the app marketplace, media storage and blogs at first. Focus on automations, AI agents and the CRM.

**About the author.** The notes cite figures for the author's client count and subscription prices; not used.

**Confidence.** High on the builder parts, which match the platform. Medium on the review example: the notes give the shape only, and the rating split raises a policy question (see `apply-map.md`).

## Section 3. Agency roadmap

**The idea.** Six stages, and the first client should cover the platform cost.

**The stages.**
1. Start: get the platform.
2. Set up: brand name, logo, domain, a template website, a Google Business Profile, a phone number, Stripe.
3. Become a builder: learn the builder, make demo automations, build a small portfolio. Use three sub-accounts: own business, demo, first client.
4. Sell: pick channels and book calls.
5. Onboard and collect: invoice, contract, add the client as a user.
6. Fulfil: build the workflows and keep the retainer.

**The sales process (structure only).**
1. First contact: the only goal is a booked call. No selling in the first message or call.
2. Discovery call: find the pain. Examples given: slow lead follow-up, no chat on the website, weak Google ranking, missed after-hours calls, no CRM.
3. Present: offer the fix and try to close on the call. If not, send a proposal.
4. Close: invoice and contract. The author drafts contracts with an AI assistant and says to have them reviewed.
5. Onboard: add the client as a staff user with limited permissions, so hidden features can be sold later. The client connects their own Google, Facebook, Instagram and accounting accounts.
6. Build: create the workflows in the client's sub-account.

**The AI assistant method.** In every sales lesson the method is the same: describe the prospect to an AI sales assistant, take its script, paste in each reply, let it write the next message.

**Confidence.** Medium. The stages are clear. The notes give no discovery questions, no proposal layout, no contract contents and no retention method beyond "keep the retainer".

## Section 5. Getting clients

**The idea.** Start with people you already know. Add one outbound channel that fits the niche. Build inbound and partnerships once there are results to show.

**The channels, as the notes compare them.**

| Channel | Best for | Upside | Downside | Working number in the notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | The first client | Trust exists | Small pool | List at least 5 owners |
| Cold calling | Businesses that answer phones | Instant feedback | Rejection, limited by hours | 100 calls per script; under 2 to 3 meetings means rewrite |
| Cold email | Larger firms with staff reading email | Volume | Low replies, spam risk, setup time | Reply rate quoted at 2 to 5% in one lesson and 1 to 3% in another |
| Cold DMs | Coaches and personal brands; corporate on LinkedIn | Free, casual | Account limits, hard to scale | A volume game |
| Networking events | High-trust fields, big cities | Easier closes in person | Slow, local | Follow up the next day |
| Inbound content | Anyone with a clear niche | Prospects raise their hand | Needs consistency | Lead-magnet posts drawing 15 to 240 comments |
| Partnerships | After case studies exist | Borrowed trust | Revenue split, slow | Two or three partners can carry it |

**Warm network (structure only).** List friends, family, former bosses and the businesses you already use. Ask for a 15 to 20 minute conversation and a free audit, not a sale. The message has three parts: what you have started doing, an offer of a few free audits with a short report, and no pressure. Three objections are covered: AI does not apply to us, no time, you are new. Afterward, send a short report of automation ideas. The first client can get a discount in exchange for a case study and testimonial. The exercise is a four-column sheet for at least five people: name, business, relationship, comfort asking.

**Cold calling.** Pull a list from map listings with a scraper extension, import it, dial from the built-in dialer. One script for the receptionist, one for the decision maker. The goal is a booked meeting.

**Cold email.** A poor fit for trades who do not sit in an inbox. The separate tutorial is mostly a pitch for the author's sending setup: many inboxes per domain, about 500 emails a day per domain, cheap lead lists. The notes cite cost and return figures; not used. The notes say the video shows only screenshots.

**Cold DMs.** Search the niche, pick the owner or manager, send a connection request with a one-line note. After they accept, open with one question about their problem. The example question asks what happens to a lead who fills a form late on a Friday night.

**Networking.** Industry and entrepreneur rooms beat general mixers. Real estate agent events are named as a good room. No rehearsed pitch: ask what they do, be interested, sell in the follow-up over coffee or a video call.

**Inbound.** Build the profile around one outcome for one niche. Post a lead magnet, ask people to comment to get it, collect name, email and phone, then open a conversation. The student example is aimed at dental clinics and missed calls.

**Partnerships.** Find a marketing agency that already serves the niche and offer to run the automation side. Two models: a referral commission, or white-label where the agency sells and keeps half. Sweeteners: first client free, priority support, regional exclusivity. Agencies ask for proof.

**Confidence.** Medium for warm network, networking and inbound: the structure is clear and the scripts are not in the notes word for word. Low for cold email: the two lessons disagree on reply rates and the tutorial is a sales pitch. None of the notes address Canadian anti-spam law, calling rules or scraped lists.

## Section 6. Free resources

**The idea.** Four documents carry content. One link imports a ready snapshot for electricians through the author's affiliate account. Eleven entries are previews of licensee-only material.

**The product playbook.**
- Stop selling custom builds. Sell one standardized automation as a subscription.
- A good product: solves a recurring problem, needs little customization, shows a measurable result fast, is simple to explain, fixes something urgent.
- Four steps: pick the automation that already worked, standardize it, make onboarding self-serve (template, short video, intake form), price it monthly.
- Sell the outcome in one line with two parts: the result, then the pain it removes.
- Stack: the platform, a connector tool, a payment tool, a video or document tool, an AI assistant.
- Product ideas: speed to lead, reviews and reputation, client onboarding, AI follow-up, chat to calendar.
- A revenue target for one product before building a second. The notes cite the figure; not used as a Next Level target.

**The guide to claiming a business profile inside an AI assistant.** The guide says the assistant's maker opened a beta that lets owners claim the profile shown for their business. Steps: sign in with a business email, search the business name exactly as on the Google listing, join the waitlist per location, verify by phone, DNS record or store platform when invited, make name, phone, website, address, hours and services match the website and Google profile exactly, check insights monthly. Per the guide it is free for now, adds a verified mark and does not raise placement. While waiting: check that the site does not block the assistant's search crawler, list services, areas, hours and booking steps on the site, keep details identical everywhere. The notes say plainly this was not verified with the maker, and that it applies directly to Ray Homes.

**Prompt writing fundamentals.** A good prompt has clarity, context, constraints and action verbs. Techniques: step-by-step instructions, chaining, iterating, assigning a role, naming the output format. Fixes: add specificity for vague output, add constraints for sprawl, rephrase when misread, ask for unique points when it repeats.

**Prompt exercises.** Graded drills: rewrite vague prompts, change role or tone, chain prompts, add constraints, split prompts with several intents, adapt one prompt across industries.

**Licensee-only previews (titles only).** A complete agency playbook, a white-labelled client onboarding course, funnel snapshots, pre-built automations, a messaging registration guide, interface branding, a missed-call text-back return calculator, dedicated support, AI chatbots (coming soon), a prompt list.

**Confidence.** High on the product playbook and the prompt documents. Low on the AI assistant profile guide: unverified. None on the previews: titles only.
