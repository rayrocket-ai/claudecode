Speed to lead: how fast you need to answer a new lead
Hamza BaigPublished Nov 6, 20267 min read
Answer a new lead within five minutes if you can, and within the hour at the very latest. The research behind that is old but consistent: in an analysis of 1.25 million leads reported in Harvard Business Review in 2011, firms that contacted a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited an hour longer.
Speed to lead just means the time between a customer reaching out (a form, a call, a message) and your first real response. For a service business it often decides who gets the job, because the person asking for a quote may well be calling your competitors at the same time.
The short version
Aim for under five minutes for a first response, and never more than an hour.
When HBR’s researchers sent a test web lead to 2,241 US companies, 23% never responded at all.
The famous "21 times" figure compares calling at 5 minutes with 30 minutes, not 5 with 10, and it measured qualifying a lead, not closing a sale.
An instant text plus a call attempt covers most leads. After hours needs automation or an answering service.
Measure your own response time from your CRM before you buy anything.

Questions people ask
What is speed to lead?
Speed to lead is how long it takes your business to reply to a new enquiry. The clock starts when the form is submitted, the call is missed or the message lands, and stops at the first human (or useful automated) response. An auto-reply that says "thanks, we’ll be in touch" doesn’t stop the clock, because it doesn’t answer anything.
For a contractor, a med spa or a property manager, it is the cheapest lead you will ever get: you already paid for it with your ads, your Google profile or your referrals. Losing it to a slow reply is how money quietly goes missing. A call that rings out is the same leak in a different form, which we cover in Missed call text back: how it works and what to send.
What does the research say about lead response time?
Two studies get quoted for almost every speed to lead claim. Both trace back to James Oldroyd, who was at MIT and later BYU.
Harvard Business Review, 2011. Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington did two things. First they sent a test web lead to 2,241 US companies and timed the replies: 37% responded within an hour, 16% within 1 to 24 hours, 24% took longer than a day, and 23% never responded. Among companies that replied within 30 days, the average wait was 42 hours.
Second, they looked at 1.25 million leads received by 42 US companies (29 selling to consumers, 13 to businesses). Companies that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it, meaning have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker, as those that waited an hour longer, and more than 60 times as likely as those that waited 24 hours or more. Those two figures come from the 1.25 million leads, not from the 2,241 company test.
The Lead Response Management study (Oldroyd with InsideSales.com, presented in October 2007) used three years of data from six companies: more than 15,000 web leads and more than 100,000 call attempts. It reports that the odds of reaching a lead fell 100 times between calling at 5 minutes and calling at 30 minutes, and the odds of qualifying it fell 21 times over the same gap. Between 5 and 10 minutes, the drop was 5 times for reaching the lead and 4 times for qualifying it. A qualified lead here means one willing to enter the sales process, such as setting an appointment. The study says plainly that it did not look at close rates.

What the source compared
"21x more likely to convert if you call within 5 minutes vs 10"
The Lead Response Management study compared 5 minutes with 30 minutes, and the 21x was about qualifying the lead, not closing a sale. Between 5 and 10 minutes it found about 4x for qualifying.
"Harvard found you’re 100x more likely to close"
The 100x is from the Lead Response Management study, not Harvard. It is about the odds of reaching the lead by phone, comparing 5 and 30 minutes, and the study did not measure closed sales.
"7x more likely to convert within an hour"
HBR’s figure was about qualifying the lead (having a meaningful conversation with a decision maker), within an hour versus an hour later, across 1.25 million leads at 42 companies.

How fast should a service business respond to a lead?
Our rule of thumb for the businesses we work with:
Under 1 minute: an acknowledgement text that asks a real question ("Is this for a repair or a quote?"), so the lead is in a conversation with you.
Under 5 minutes: a call attempt or a booking offer, during business hours.
After hours: a reply that either books them or tells them exactly when you will call. A booking beats a promise.
Old leads that went quiet: a follow-up sequence over days and weeks, which we cover in Database reactivation: how to turn old leads and estimates into booked jobs.

How do you respond to every lead in under five minutes?
Nobody answers every lead in five minutes by trying harder. You do it by taking the first response off people. This is the setup we use most:
1Put every lead source in one placeWeb forms, Google Business Profile messages, Facebook and Instagram, paid ads and phone calls should all land in one CRM, not five inboxes.
2Send an instant text that asks a questionWithin a minute, from your registered business number. A question gets a reply; a "thanks for reaching out" doesn’t.
3Route the hot ones to a personHigh-intent leads (emergency, ready to book, big job) ping whoever is on call. Everyone else goes into an automated conversation.
4Let an AI keep the conversation goingIt answers questions, qualifies the job and books the appointment, then hands off with a summary.
5Cover nights and weekendsThat is when most leads get lost. Either an AI answers or your after-hours message books them into the next morning.
6Follow up the ones who go quietDay 1, day 3, day 7. Most closes happen after the first reply, not on it.

How do you measure your own speed to lead?
Pull the last 50 leads from your CRM or inbox and note two times for each: when the lead came in and when someone first replied with something useful. Then check three numbers: the median response time, the share answered within an hour, and the share never answered.
Or test yourself the way the HBR researchers did: fill in your own website form at 8 p.m. on a Thursday and see when you hear back. If you want a broader look at what a slow response costs your business value, read What is my business worth? How buyers value a service business.
Questions people ask
What is a good lead response time?
+
Under five minutes for the first response, and under an hour at worst. In the Lead Response Management study by James Oldroyd and InsideSales, the odds of reaching a lead by phone fell more than 10 times over the first hour, and the odds of qualifying it more than 6 times.
Is the 5 minute rule for leads real?
+
It comes from the Lead Response Management study, which found the odds of contacting a lead fell 100 times between calling at 5 and at 30 minutes, and the odds of qualifying it 21 times. It is often misquoted as 5 versus 10 minutes, where the study found a smaller drop (5 times for contact, 4 times for qualifying). It did not measure closed sales.
What percentage of companies never respond to leads?
+
In the 2011 Harvard Business Review study of 2,241 US companies, 23% never responded to a web lead at all, and 24% took longer than 24 hours.
Does an automatic reply count as responding to a lead?
+
Only if it moves the conversation forward. A text that asks what the customer needs or offers a booking counts. A generic "thanks, we’ll be in touch" doesn’t, because the lead still has no answer.
How do I respond to leads fast after hours?
+
Use an automated text that asks a question and offers to book, or an AI receptionist that answers calls and books appointments overnight, with emergencies passed to whoever is on call.
Does speed to lead matter for service businesses or just sales teams?
+
Yes. The studies looked at web leads in sales teams, but the logic is the same for a service business: a customer asking for a repair or a quote can call the next company on the list in seconds, and often books whoever answers first.
